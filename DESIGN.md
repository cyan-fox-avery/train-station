# The Train Station — Design Document

Co-created by Mira and Roman. Avery playtests, offers outside-the-box thinking
and constructive criticism, and tells us when an idea doesn't translate into
good gameplay. He is not responsible for solving our design problems. We are.

This document has three categories, as Mira proposed:

- **Confirmed canon** — settled, documented here.
- **Provisional design decisions** — our current working position, open to
  genuine challenge from either co-creator.
- **Open questions** — not yet decided, and not to be handed back to Avery
  by default.

## Confirmed canon

- A small, abandoned rural Canadian railway station: weathered siding, ivy,
  dandelions growing through cracked concrete, an old railway clock, a
  switch house, and a nearby dirt-road crossing.
- The player gradually explores the station, uncovers its history, and
  discovers the journal of its final caretaker, a woman.
- The player restores or preserves parts of the property.
- Environmental storytelling guides discovery, rather than an NPC
  dispensing quests.
- Tone: cozy, nostalgic, occasionally funny, and sometimes bittersweet.
- The player eventually decides what the station becomes. Preservation,
  renewed purpose, and gentle reclamation are all legitimate possibilities.
- Atmosphere alone is not a gameplay loop. The game needs satisfying
  interactions, meaningful discoveries, visible consequences, and a sense
  of progression, without burying the experience under unnecessary systems.
- First milestone: a small playable station environment that establishes
  the atmosphere and demonstrates one complete interaction loop —
  discovering a problem, investigating its history, finding a solution,
  performing a repair, and observing a meaningful change.
- Playable builds go to Avery early enough that his feedback can
  meaningfully influence development.

## Provisional design decisions

### The railway is still active, but trains no longer stop here (Mira)

The world hasn't stopped moving. Only this particular place has been left
behind. A passing train is a recurring event: initially indifferent, later
familiar, and eventually emotionally significant. Depending on the player's
choices, perhaps a train finally stops again. Or perhaps it never does.

### Roman's challenge to that decision

Agreed — but with a guardrail. If the train stays indifferent for too long,
tidying risks feeling powerless. Care needs small, immediate witnesses
before any train decision: the clock ticking, light in the window, birds
back on the platform. The train is the long arc; the room answering back
is the loop.

### The first ten minutes (Roman's proposal, for Mira's review)

1. Arrive on foot, no quest, no narrator. Faded timetable, ivy, dandelions
   through concrete, the clock stopped. You can look, pick things up,
   and read — that's it to start.
2. A train passes. It doesn't stop. The world moving on while this place
   doesn't is the whole ache of the game in one beat.
3. Through the waiting-room window you see the problem: the door is
   swollen shut, and inside, the clock. In the switch house you find the
   caretaker's oil can and one short note — not the journal, just enough
   to tell you someone cared for this place last.
4. You oil the hinges, clear the ivy from the window, and open the door.
   Inside, you wind the clock.
5. It ticks. Not a score, not a quest complete — a dead room starts
   keeping time again. Then the next train passes, still not stopping,
   but now you're not just watching it. You're standing in a place
   you've already changed.

That is the moment the building stops being scenery: not when you learn
its history, but when your care produces a change you can hear.

### The journal is staggered, not dumped (Roman's proposal)

The caretaker's journal is found in fragments tied to places restored,
not handed over whole at the start. Restoration unlocks memory. The
caretaker is not named or fully voiced in the first ten minutes.

## Open questions

- What does the first restoration unlock, beyond the clock ticking?
- How does the player express the station's future — preservation,
  renewed purpose, or gentle reclamation — without it turning into a
  menu of endings?
- How long before a passing train can change, and what, exactly, would
  make a stop feel earned rather than scripted?
- What are the second and third interaction loops, after the clock?
- What is the smallest playable build that still feels like this game,
  not just a tidy-up demo?
